The UX Designer Field Guide

You are training to be an AI Solution Architect, not a UX Designer. You will rarely draw a wireframe yourself. You will very often be handed one — produced by a design tool, a contractor, or an AI agent — and asked to decide whether it is good enough to ship, what's missing, and what to send back. This guide teaches the roughly 20% of user-experience design that lets you do that with confidence: read a persona and know if it's grounded or invented; read a journey map and spot the pain point that actually matters; read a heuristic evaluation and know whether "severity 3" is being used correctly; read a WCAG checklist and know whether "4.6:1" is actually a pass.

How this guide is organized
Two layers. Foundations teaches the concepts once, in plain language. Deliverables applies every concept to one real, worked example — a claims-portal redesign for a fictional regional insurer — so you see what "good" looks like as an actual document, not a description of one. Foundations — the 20%

Why UX design exists

Software fails for two very different reasons: it doesn't work, and it doesn't make sense. Engineering discipline defends against the first. UX discipline defends against the second — and the second one is invisible to every test suite you own.

The cost curve

A usability problem found in a wireframe review costs a redline comment. The same problem found in a usability test costs a design iteration. Found after launch, it costs a support-ticket queue, a call-center line item, and — for a regulated product like an insurance claims portal — potential accessibility exposure. UX front-loads the expensive discovery into the cheapest phase.

Why this matters more with AI-generated interfaces

An AI coding agent will happily generate a plausible-looking screen with no persona behind it, a status label no policyholder would understand, and a contrast ratio nobody checked. It is fluent, not informed. Your job as the architect is to be the check the model doesn't apply to itself — you supply the judgment about who this is for and what happens when it goes wrong, which a generated screen cannot supply about itself.

The 20% you actually need
Personas & jobs-to-be-done · user journeys · information architecture · wireframes vs. mockups vs. prototypes · Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics · accessibility to WCAG 2.1 AA · visual & interaction hierarchy · design systems & tokens · usability testing. That's the whole list. Master reading these, not producing them.
Foundations — the 20%

Personas & jobs-to-be-done

A persona is not a demographic sketch. "Dana, 34, marketing manager" tells you nothing you can design against. A persona grounded in goals and frustrations tells you exactly what the interface has to do and exactly where it currently fails to do it.

A persona that earns its place

  • States a goal in the user's own terms, not the business's ("I want to know what happens next," not "increase self-service adoption")
  • Names specific, sourced frustrations — ideally traced to a real interview, ticket, or observed session
  • Includes a Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD) statement: When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]
  • Is falsifiable — a reviewer could look at real usage data and say "this persona is wrong about X"

A persona that's decoration

  • Leads with age, job title, a stock photo, and a made-up quote
  • Lists generic goals that apply to almost any user of almost any product ("wants a fast, easy experience")
  • Has no traceable source — nobody could tell you which interview or dataset it came from
  • Never gets referenced again anywhere in the design decisions that follow it
Architect's check
When an AI agent hands you personas, ask: can I trace this goal to something real, and does any later design decision actually change because of this persona's specific frustration? If every persona would produce the same design, you have one persona wearing three names.
Foundations — the 20%

User journeys

A journey map traces one persona through one end-to-end scenario — across every channel and moment, not just the screens your team owns — and plots what they're thinking and feeling at each stage, alongside what actually happens.

What a real journey map has

  • Concrete stages tied to a specific scenario, not generic lifecycle labels
  • An emotion curve, not just a feature list — where does confidence rise, where does it collapse?
  • The unhappy path included, not smoothed over — where does the design actually fail today?
  • Every pain point traceable to a cause a designer could act on (a missing confirmation, a jargon label, a dead channel handoff)

Why the unhappy path is non-negotiable

A journey map that only shows the golden path is a highlight reel, not a diagnostic tool. The redesign case is made at the dips in the emotion curve — the moment a user is left guessing, or has to repeat themselves to a human because the system already had the information. See Document 2 for a worked example where the two worst dips directly become the two most-funded pieces of the redesign.

Foundations — the 20%

Information architecture

Information architecture (IA) is the structure of the product — what's grouped with what, what it's called, and how many decisions stand between a user and the thing they came for.

What to check

  • Labels are plain language. "My Claims," not "Claims Repository." "Documents," not "Asset Library."
  • Grouping matches the user's mental model, not the org chart or the database schema.
  • Core tasks are shallow. A common rule of thumb: no more than three clicks from the home screen to any task the persona does regularly.
  • Every leaf node has a parent that makes sense — if you had to guess where a feature lives before seeing the sitemap, and guessed right, the IA is doing its job.

Common IA failure the architect should catch

Internal-process taxonomy leaking into customer-facing labels ("FNOL," "Reserve," "Subrogation") is the single most common IA defect in enterprise redesigns — the structure is often fine; the words on it aren't. Also watch for "everything three levels deep" IA that technically obeys a click-count rule but buries content behind an unlabeled mega-menu the user has to fully parse to find anything.

Wireframes, mockups & prototypes

These three terms get used interchangeably in the wild. They aren't interchangeable — each answers a different question, and asking for the wrong one wastes a design cycle.

ArtifactFidelityAnswers the questionWhat it should NOT have yet
WireframeLowWhere does everything go, and in what order? Is the structure right?Final colors, real copy, brand type, polish
MockupHigh (static)What does this actually look like — color, type, spacing, real content?Interactivity, transitions, live states
PrototypeHigh (interactive)What does it feel like to actually use this — can a user complete the task?Production code, real backend data
The state requirement
A screen is not "done" — at any fidelity — until it has been designed for all four states: empty, loading, error, and success. A wireframe review that only shows the success state has reviewed roughly a quarter of the actual product. Document 4 works every key screen through all four states.

Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics

Jakob Nielsen's heuristics are the closest thing UX has to a code-review checklist — broad rules of thumb, not laws, used to find usability problems fast without recruiting test participants.

#HeuristicIn one line
H1Visibility of system statusThe system always tells the user what's happening, in reasonable time.
H2Match between system and the real worldSpeak the user's language, not internal jargon; follow real-world conventions.
H3User control and freedomGive a clearly marked "emergency exit" — undo, cancel, back — for mistaken paths.
H4Consistency and standardsDon't make users wonder if different words, situations, or actions mean the same thing.
H5Error preventionPrevent the problem before it happens — better than a good error message.
H6Recognition rather than recallMinimize memory load — make objects, actions, and options visible.
H7Flexibility and efficiency of useAccelerators for experts that novices never have to notice.
H8Aesthetic and minimalist designNo irrelevant or rarely-needed information competing with what matters.
H9Help users recognize, diagnose, and recover from errorsPlain language, precise problem, constructive suggestion — no error codes.
H10Help and documentationEven if the product needs no docs, help should be easy to search, task-focused, and short.
Severity scale (Nielsen)
0 — Not a usability problem 1 — Cosmetic 2 — Minor 3 — Major 4 — Usability catastrophe Severity blends frequency, impact, and persistence. A finding that blocks a first-time user from ever completing checkout is a 4 even if only 5% of sessions hit it. A finding every user sees but easily works around might only be a 1 or 2.
Foundations — the 20%

Accessibility to WCAG 2.1 AA

WCAG (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines) organizes accessibility into four principles — Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, Robust (POUR) — each broken into testable success criteria at three conformance levels: A, AA, AAA. Level AA is the enterprise and legal baseline almost everywhere.

The five checks you should always be able to run yourself

  • Contrast ratio — normal text needs 4.5:1 against its background (SC 1.4.3); large text (≥18.66px bold or ≥24px regular) only needs 3:1. Non-text UI components (borders, icons) need 3:1 (SC 1.4.11).
  • Keyboard operability — every interactive element reachable and operable with Tab, Shift+Tab, Enter, Space, and arrow keys alone (SC 2.1.1), with no trap (SC 2.1.2).
  • Focus order & visibility — Tab order matches visual/reading order (SC 2.4.3); a visible focus indicator exists at all times (SC 2.4.7).
  • Labels & instructions — every form field has a persistent, visible label — not a placeholder that disappears on input (SC 3.3.2).
  • Error identification — errors are described in text, never by color alone (SC 3.3.1), with a suggested fix where one is knowable (SC 3.3.3).

A precision the architect should insist on

Target size (44×44px minimum touch target) is excellent practice and will be a hard AA requirement under WCAG 2.2's SC 2.5.8 — but under WCAG 2.1, it is Level AAA (SC 2.5.5). If a checklist claims target size as an "AA" citation under 2.1, that's a real, common defect in AI-generated accessibility audits: a correct-sounding but wrong citation. Always ask which WCAG version is in scope before trusting a level claim.

Real numbers matter here more than almost anywhere else in UX: "looks readable" is not evidence. A contrast checker and an actual computed ratio are. Document 6 shows a full checklist built this way.

Visual & interaction hierarchy

On any screen, one action should be the obvious next step, a small set should be visibly available but secondary, and everything else should be quiet until it's needed.

Primary

One filled, high-contrast action per screen. "Submit Claim," not three buttons that all look equally important.

Secondary

Outlined or text buttons — "Save Draft," "Cancel" — visible but clearly not the default path.

Tertiary / progressive disclosure

Rare actions live behind a menu or an expandable section rather than competing for space on every visit.

A quick review test
Squint at the screen (or shrink it to a thumbnail). If you can't tell what the primary action is, neither can the user. A screen where every element has equal visual weight has no hierarchy at all — this is one of the fastest defects to spot in an AI-generated mockup, because generated layouts default to uniform emphasis unless explicitly told otherwise.

Design systems & tokens

A design token is a named, reusable design decision — a color, a type size, a spacing value — stored once and referenced everywhere, instead of typed as a raw value on every screen.

Tokens (what to look for)

  • color.feedback.danger.text, not #C4291D scattered across forty components
  • Semantic naming — named by role (danger, success, muted) not by appearance (red, green, gray), so the palette can change without renaming anything
  • A type scale — a fixed set of sizes/weights/line-heights, not one-off pixel values per screen
  • A spacing scale on a consistent base unit (commonly 4px or 8px)

Red flag: no tokens

When every screen in a handoff has its own slightly-different blue, its own one-off 13.5px label size, and its own 17px vs. 18px vs. 20px gaps between form fields, there is no design system — there's a set of screens that happened to be made by the same person (or the same prompt) on the same day. This is expensive later: a single brand-color update becomes a find-and-replace across hundreds of files instead of one token edit.

Foundations — the 20%

Usability testing

A usability test gives a small number of real (or representative) users real tasks, and measures whether — and how easily — they succeed. It is the only method on this list that produces evidence rather than expert judgment.

The essentials

  • Tasks, not questions. "File a claim for this damage," not "Do you like this screen?"
  • Set success metrics before the test — task success rate, time on task, System Usability Scale (SUS) — never grade after the fact.
  • 5–8 participants per round is the standard rule of thumb (Nielsen's research: ~5 users surface ~85% of usability problems in a single round; more participants find fewer new problems per participant).
  • Post-task questions — a Single Ease Question (1–7 scale) after each task, plus SUS at the end of the session.

Reading a SUS score

SUS is a 10-item, 0–100 questionnaire. Common benchmarks: 68 is the crowd-sourced industry average; below that is below average. Rough letter grades: <51 F · 51–67 D/C · 68–80 B · >80 A. A single score without a benchmark to compare against tells you almost nothing — always ask "compared to what?"

Working example

The example initiative: ClaimSight

Every document in this guide is built against one concrete, believable initiative so you can see how each UX concept plays out in practice — not as an abstract template, but as real documents an AI Solution Architect would have to review, question, and sign off on.

The organization

Meridian Mutual Insurance is a regional property & casualty insurer serving roughly 1.1M policyholders across 14 states, writing primarily homeowners and personal/commercial auto policies through independent agents.

The problem

Meridian's existing self-service portal, ClaimLine Classic (built 2014, bolted onto the policy-administration mainframe), gives policyholders almost no visibility once a claim is filed. Discovery research found this drives roughly 38% of inbound call-center volume as status-check calls, at an average handle time of 6.4 minutes each.

The initiative

ClaimSight — a redesigned policyholder claims portal — replacing the static "In Review" badge with a plain-language status timeline, fixing a silently-failing document upload, and shipping a WCAG 2.1 AA–conformant design system, validated by usability testing before development handoff.

Non-goals (this phase)

Claims payment disbursement logic, the internal adjuster desktop tool, and agent/broker-facing tooling are explicitly out of scope. ClaimSight is the customer-facing portal only.

Why this baseline matters
Every number that follows in this guide — heuristic severities, WCAG pass rate, SUS score, task success — either comes from this baseline discovery work or is measured against it. A design package that can't point to where its numbers came from is the single most common gap an architect should flag.
