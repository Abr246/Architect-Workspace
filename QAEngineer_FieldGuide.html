Field Guide

The QA Engineer Field Guide

You are training to become an AI Solution Architect. You will not spend your career hand-writing every test case — AI will draft most of the suite in seconds. Your job is to direct, evaluate, and approve the tests and quality evidence AI produces, and to make the ship/no-ship call that follows from it. This guide teaches the roughly 20% of quality-assurance discipline that makes that judgment possible, then shows what "good" looks like by building a complete, realistic QA package for one example initiative end to end.

The Example Initiative

ClaimSight AI — Meridian Mutual Insurance

A regional P&C carrier licensed across 9 states. ClaimSight AI ingests First Notice of Loss (FNOL) claims from the policyholder web/mobile app, the independent-agent portal, and a call center; computes a composite fraud-risk score (a bought SIU rules engine blended with an in-house ML overlay); and routes each claim to Fast-Track auto-adjudication, Standard Adjuster Review, or a Special Investigations Unit (SIU) referral. Release 3.2 — "Automated Triage & SIU Referral Routing" is the release under test in this guide.

Business rules under test

  • BR-01 (Fast-Track): estimated loss < $2,000 and composite fraud-risk score < 20 → auto-adjudicate, no adjuster required.
  • BR-02 (SIU referral): composite fraud-risk score ≥ 70 → publish SIUReferralCreated, route to the SIU queue, regardless of claim amount.
  • BR-03 (Standard Review): everything else → the Standard Adjuster Review queue.
Why a threshold exists at all
The Solution Architecture Document's Risk Register (RISK-09) requires the AI to never auto-decide above a claim-value threshold — the adjuster workbench always shows AI output as advisory above it. BR-01 is that threshold, made concrete and testable.

What QA is verifying

  • Claims Ingestion Service validation (required fields, VIN format, policy status)
  • Fraud Risk Scoring Service latency and graceful degradation (NFR-03, circuit breaker)
  • Triage routing correctness against BR-01/BR-02/BR-03, including every boundary
  • Idempotent event consumers under Kafka's at-least-once delivery (NFR-07)
  • Audit trail completeness (NFR-10) and PII handling in logs
  • WCAG 2.1 AA conformance of the rebuilt FNOL and status-tracking flows
Glossary
FNOL — First Notice of Loss. SIU — Special Investigations Unit. ACL — Anti-Corruption Layer (the service that isolates ClaimSight from the legacy ClaimCore system). CAT — Catastrophe (weather) event. NFR — Non-Functional Requirement.
Foundations — the 20%

Why QA exists

LLMs are probabilistic; production systems must be deterministic. QA is the discipline that closes that gap — it converts "the AI says it works" into "here is the evidence it works, and here is what happens when it doesn't."

Without QA discipline

An AI hands you a claims-triage service with a confident narrative: "all tests pass." Nobody asked whether a duplicate Kafka delivery creates two SIU referrals, whether a $2,000.00 claim is Fast-Track or Standard, or whether the fraud-score fallback actually degrades instead of hanging. The system that demos perfectly fails on the first real edge case — usually in production, in front of a regulator or a policyholder.

With QA discipline

Every requirement traces to at least one test. Boundary values, failure paths, and duplicate-delivery scenarios are tested on purpose, not discovered by accident. Severity and priority are argued, not assumed. The release decision is a documented recommendation backed by pass/fail evidence — not a feeling.

Your job as the architect
You will rarely write the 24 test cases in this guide's Document 3 yourself — an AI agent will draft most of them. Your job is to know what a complete set looks like well enough to spot what an AI-generated set is missing: usually the negative paths, the boundaries, and the duplicate-delivery cases, because those are the least "impressive" to generate and the most likely to be skipped.

Strategy vs. plan vs. cases

Three documents, three altitudes. AI-generated QA packages routinely collapse all three into one document — that collapse is itself a defect an architect should catch.

Common AI-generation failure
Asking an AI for "a test plan" often returns a document that mixes all three altitudes — a paragraph of strategy, a table of cases, no schedule. Ask for each separately, and check that the strategy document doesn't quietly contain release dates (those belong in the plan) and the plan doesn't contain step-by-step test scripts (those belong in the cases).

The testing pyramid

The healthy shape of a test suite: many fast, cheap tests at the base; fewer, slower, more expensive tests at the top. A suite that inverts this — more end-to-end than unit tests — is a process violation, not a stylistic choice: it's slow to run, flaky, and expensive to maintain.

The testing pyramid, with ClaimSight AI's Release 3.2 suite mapped onto it.
Review habit
If an AI-generated suite is heavy on end-to-end browser tests and light on unit-level boundary tests, that's an inverted pyramid — it will be "impressive" in a demo and expensive and flaky in CI. Ask for the boundary and negative cases to move down to the fastest layer that can express them.

Functional vs. non-functional testing

Functional testing asks "does it do the right thing?" Non-functional testing asks "does it do the right thing well enough — fast enough, safely enough, accessibly enough?" Both are mandatory; non-functional tests without a measurable numeric target are not tests, they're opinions.

Functional

Correctness of behavior against a requirement.

Routing logic (BR-01/02/03)Field validationAudit logging

Non-functional

Quality attributes, each with a number.

Performance: ≤2.0s p95Security: 0 unredacted PIIAccessibility: 0 critical WCAG violations
Reject this claim
"The fraud scoring service is fast." That is not a test result. "Fraud scoring returns a composite score in ≤2.0s p95 / ≤4.0s p99 under 500 claims/min, measured across a 6-hour load test" (NFR-03) is. If an AI hands you the first sentence as evidence, send it back for the second.

Given/When/Then acceptance tests

A precise, three-part shape for an acceptance test: the starting state, the action, and the verifiable outcome. It forces every test to name its preconditions instead of assuming them.

TC-008BOUNDARY
GIVEN a validated FNOL claim with a composite fraud-risk score of exactly 70
WHEN the Triage Service evaluates routing rules (BR-01/BR-02/BR-03)
THEN the claim is routed to the SIU queue and a SIUReferralCreated event is published, because BR-02's threshold is "≥ 70"
Why this shape catches AI hallucination
An AI asked to "test the SIU routing" will happily write a test for score 85. It is far less likely to write the boundary at exactly 70 unless the G/W/T format forces it to state the precondition precisely — "exactly 70" is a different sentence than "a high score," and writing the sentence out loud surfaces the boundary.

Positive, negative, boundary & idempotency

Four case types every requirement needs to be tested against, not just the first one.

Positive (happy path)

Valid input, expected outcome. TC-001: a clean FNOL, low fraud score, low amount → Fast-Track.

Negative (failure path)

Invalid input, must be rejected cleanly. TC-010: missing policy number → validation error, no claim created.

Boundary

Values exactly at, and one unit either side of, a threshold. TC-004/005: $2,000.00 vs. $1,999.99.

Idempotency

The same operation, replayed, must not duplicate its side effects. TC-016: a redelivered ClaimReceived event must not create a second claim.

The bar for "complete"
Per this repo's own idempotency rule, every side-effecting operation must be safe to run twice — see Document 3, TC-016 through TC-018. A suite with only positive and negative cases, no boundary or idempotency cases, is not done, no matter how many tests it contains.

Traceability & coverage

Traceability means every requirement has at least one test, and every test traces back to a requirement — no orphans in either direction. Coverage measures how thoroughly, and at what layer.

Orphan requirement

A requirement with zero tests. Nobody would know if it silently broke. See Document 4 — ClaimSight AI's 15 requirements each have at least one traced test.

Orphan test

A test that doesn't map to any requirement — usually a sign the AI invented behavior nobody specified. See the Coverage Matrix (Document 7) callout on REQ-009's single-test coverage for the opposite failure mode: a requirement that's technically covered but thinly.

Coverage % can mislead
"100% requirements coverage" sounds complete. It can mean every requirement has exactly one test — including a high-risk requirement resting on a single manual check. Coverage tells you a test exists; it does not tell you the test is proportional to the risk. See the Architect's Review Lens below.

Severity vs. priority

Two independent axes, routinely conflated. Severity is technical impact — how badly is the system broken? Priority is business urgency — how soon must it be fixed? A defect can be high on one axis and low on the other.

Bad pattern
Marking every defect "Critical" to get attention starves the triage process of signal — see Document 5 for the fully worked example and the full defect log in Document 6.

Regression vs. exploratory testing

Regression

Re-running existing tests after a change to confirm nothing that used to work broke. Scripted, repeatable, automatable. Every fix in Document 5 triggers a targeted regression run before it's marked Closed.

Exploratory

Unscripted, skilled investigation without a predefined script — following a hunch. DEF-001, DEF-003, and DEF-005 in this guide were all found this way, not by a written test case. A pure automated suite, however complete on paper, still misses what a curious human tester finds by poking at the real system.

Both are required
Regression proves the past still works. Exploratory testing finds what nobody thought to specify. A "complete" suite that is 100% scripted and never explored the running system is missing a whole category of defect.

Shift-left testing

Find a defect during design review and it costs a redline comment. Find the same defect in production and it costs an incident, a hotfix, and — for a regulated insurer — possible regulator exposure. Shift-left means testing activities move as early as possible: reviewing requirements for testability, writing acceptance criteria before code exists, and running static/contract checks in CI before a human ever clicks a button.

Where ClaimSight AI shifted left
REQ-002's VIN-format rule was written as a testable G/W/T acceptance criterion during requirements review — before the Claims Ingestion Service was built — which is exactly how DEF-004 (a case-sensitivity bug in that same rule) was caught by an automated test (TC-013) instead of by a policyholder submitting a bad VIN in production.
