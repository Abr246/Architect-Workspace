The Business Analyst Field Guide

You are training to become an AI Solution Architect. You will not spend your career writing requirements documents by hand — AI will draft most of them. Your job is to direct, evaluate, and approve what the AI produces. This guide teaches the roughly 20% of business analysis discipline that makes that judgment possible, then shows you what "good" looks like by building a complete, realistic requirements package for one example initiative end to end.

Working example

The example initiative: Harvest AI

Every document in this guide is built against one concrete, believable initiative so you can see how each discipline plays out in practice — not as an abstract template, but as a real decision an AI Solution Architect would have to sign off on.

The company

Basil & Char Kitchens is a 22-unit fast-casual restaurant chain across the Southeast United States, $58M in annual revenue, known for made-to-order grain bowls and sandwiches built from locally sourced ingredients.

The problem

Every Sunday night, each store manager spends roughly 5.5 hours manually reviewing spreadsheet sales reports and placing a supplier order by feel. The result: food waste running at 11.4% of food cost (industry benchmark 4–6%), stockouts on 6.2% of menu-item-days, and 18% of orders placed as costly emergency "rush" orders at a 22% price premium.

The initiative

Harvest AI — an AI-powered demand forecasting and inventory automation platform that predicts item-level daily demand per store, auto-drafts supplier purchase orders for manager approval, and continuously monitors mid-shift sales pace to catch stockout risk before it happens.

Scope

Phase 1: a 12-week pilot across 5 stores in the Southeast region. Phase 2: full rollout to all 22 stores. Sponsor: Chief Operating Officer. Business owner: VP of Culinary Operations.

Why this example
Restaurant demand forecasting has everything an AI Solution Architect will meet repeatedly: a measurable business problem, a human-in-the-loop AI workflow, real integration boundaries (POS, supplier ordering), financial-value math, and a governance question (how much should the AI be allowed to do without a human sign-off?).
Foundations 1 / 9

Why business analysis exists

Business analysis exists to close the gap between what the business needs and what gets built. Left unmanaged, that gap produces systems that are technically correct and commercially useless — a forecasting model with 95% accuracy that nobody uses because the order screen takes eleven taps, or a beautifully engineered API that automates the wrong decision.

As an AI Solution Architect, generative AI will draft your requirements, user stories, and even your process diagrams in seconds. That collapses the writing cost of business analysis to nearly zero — and raises the judgment cost to nearly everything. You are no longer graded on how fast you can write a BRD. You are graded on whether you can look at an AI-generated BRD and know, specifically, what is wrong with it before it reaches a store manager, a supplier integration, or a compliance officer.

The 20% that matters
You do not need to become a full-time business analyst. You need the ~20% of the discipline that lets you direct the AI ("draft acceptance criteria for the order-override story, and don't forget the $500 escalation rule"), evaluate its output ("this requirement isn't testable — reject it"), and approve what ships. That 20% is what the rest of this Foundations section teaches.

Functional vs. non-functional requirements

Functional requirements (FR) describe what the system does — the behaviors, calculations, and workflows a user or another system can observe. Non-functional requirements (NFR) describe how well the system does it — the quality attributes that make a functionally correct system actually usable in production: performance, availability, security, accuracy, auditability, scalability.

Functional — "what"

The system shall generate a draft purchase order from the approved forecast, mapped to the store's supplier catalog SKUs.

Non-functional — "how well"

The nightly forecast batch job for all 22 stores shall complete within 45 minutes end-to-end.

Common AI-generation failure
AI drafting tools routinely produce long lists of functional requirements and zero non-functional requirements, because NFRs require domain judgment about failure modes, not just a description of the happy path. A BRD with FRs but no NFRs is incomplete by definition — see the Harvest AI BRD for a fully paired example.

User stories & acceptance criteria (Given / When / Then)

A user story captures a requirement from the perspective of the person who benefits from it, in the form: "As a <role>, I want <capability>, so that <business value>." The story is deliberately short — it is a placeholder for a conversation, not a specification.

Acceptance criteria are what turn that placeholder into something testable. The Given / When / Then format states the precondition, the triggering action, and the expected, observable outcome:

Given an item-store pair with fewer than 90 days of transaction history,
When the forecast is generated,
Then the forecast line is labeled "Low Confidence" and excluded from any auto-approval workflow.

A story without acceptance criteria is an opinion. A story with Given/When/Then criteria is a contract a QA engineer — human or AI — can test without asking anyone what "done" means. See the full set in User Stories.

MoSCoW prioritization

MoSCoW forces an explicit priority on every requirement so scope decisions are made deliberately, not by whoever shouts loudest in the room:

MUST

Non-negotiable for launch. Without it, the release has no business value or is unsafe to ship.

SHOULD

Important, high value, but the release can go live without it if truly necessary.

COULD

Desirable, low cost of omission. First thing cut when the schedule is squeezed.

WON'T

Explicitly out of scope for this release — recorded so it isn't silently re-litigated later.

Reviewer's flag
If an AI-generated requirements list marks everything "Must," reject it and ask for a forced ranking. "Everything is a Must" is not a priority — it's the absence of one. A healthy Must-list should typically be under half of total scope; see the priority mix chart in the BRD.

Personas & use cases

Personas are composite, realistic profiles of the people who will use or be affected by the system — built around their goals and frustrations, not demographics. A persona answers "what is this person trying to accomplish, and what currently gets in their way?" See the four Harvest AI personas here.

Use cases describe a complete interaction between an actor and the system to achieve a goal, including a numbered main flow and the alternate and exception flows — what happens when the supplier is out of stock, when the weather API is down, when the manager doesn't respond in time. A user story says what is wanted; a use case walks through how the interaction actually unfolds, happy path and unhappy path both. See Use Cases.

Traceability

Traceability is the discipline of linking every requirement forward to the story or test that verifies it, and backward to the business objective that justified it. A Requirements Traceability Matrix (RTM) is the artifact that makes those links explicit and auditable.

Why it matters to an architect
An "orphan requirement" — one with no linked test case, no linked story, or no linked business objective — is a requirement nobody can prove was built correctly, and nobody can prove is still needed. When you approve an AI-generated RTM, every single row must resolve end-to-end. See the full Requirements Traceability Matrix.

KPIs that matter

Requirements exist to move a small number of numbers. For Harvest AI, an architect should be able to name the four KPIs cold — food waste %, stockout rate, forecast accuracy (MAPE), and manager hours reclaimed — because every requirement in the BRD should trace back to moving one of them. See them live in the Executive Summary.
